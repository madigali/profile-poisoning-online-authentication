# 6. Conclusion

Accepted-only online adaptation couples access decisions to the evidence available for future learning. In the evaluated score-profile systems, this created two related effects: accepted impostor observations could enter the adaptive state, and rejected genuine observations were withheld from it. At the historical reference operating points, score-informed boundary poisoning produced large Blind EMA increases in held-out attack success on HMOG and McGill. Guarded EMA used a separate update-authorization condition and kept the reference-point aggregate ASR close to Frozen.

These effects were not invariant under recalibration. Blind amplification narrowed at nominal 5%, and its mean nominal-1% effect was negative on both datasets for the tested boundary strategy. On the matched strict HMOG cohort, however, Blind increased FRR by 6.89 percentage points and HTER by 3.22 points. Lower attack success therefore did not establish harmless updating or general poisoning resistance. Recalibration changed the initial threshold, its associated \(k\), boundary candidates, and feasible paths. The Guarded margin was also state dependent: it added no restriction when the authentication threshold reached or exceeded the enrollment mean.

The temporal experiments provided no deployable recovery solution. Guarded did not improve the primary late-FRR endpoint, while label-aware Oracle-all changed trajectories and lowered FRR at the cost of higher FAR. TQ-EMA v1 missed its recovery criterion, and TQ-EMA v2 and Quantile stopped at validation without outer estimates. Under these protocols, adaptive authentication should be evaluated through joint reporting of post-update ASR or FAR, FRR, calibration transfer, update composition, gate activity, and attack feasibility. The findings motivate testing bounded recovery mechanisms that obtain trusted evidence partly independently of the current access decision.
