# 6. Conclusion

Online adaptation is intended to keep behavioral authentication responsive to temporal change, but it also turns operational decisions into inputs to the future authentication state. This study examined that coupling through four connected questions: whether accepted impostor observations can poison a score-level profile, whether update authorization can reduce that exposure, whether accepted-only learning retains the evidence required for genuine temporal recovery, and whether recovery alternatives can improve this balance under frozen security constraints. The evaluation combined a controlled HMOG mechanism study with an independent two-day McGill replication rather than treating security and adaptation utility as separate tasks.

The poisoning experiments showed that Blind EMA was vulnerable to the score-informed boundary strategy. At the maximum tested budget, \(K=8\), HMOG post-poison ASR increased from 22.92% for Frozen to 53.96% for Blind EMA. Because attack candidates and future probes were separated before selection and the profile was frozen before probe evaluation, this increase reflects a change in later decisions rather than reuse of the poisoning observations. Guarded EMA retained the authentication threshold while imposing a separate update-authorization condition; its ASR was 22.93% in the same comparison. The corresponding direction was reproduced on McGill. These results support separate update authorization as a security control under the tested protocols, but they do not show that a confidence gate eliminates poisoning or that the carried-forward gate is universally optimal.

The clean temporal experiments exposed the complementary adaptation cost. During a downward claimant-score shift, accepted-only policies excluded rejected genuine observations from learning and retained a higher-scoring subset. Guarded profile trajectories therefore did not follow the full genuine-score movement, and Guarded did not preserve positive late-FRR utility relative to Frozen. Oracle-all-genuine restored every genuine adaptation observation and changed the profile trajectory in the expected downward direction. In claimant-only HMOG at \(D=2\), it lowered late FRR by 6.60 percentage points relative to Frozen, but late FAR increased by 6.08 points. Oracle-all is non-deployable because it requires ground-truth genuine labels. Its value is diagnostic: the comparison supports selective-update bias caused by accepted-only feedback censoring as a contributor to weak recovery, while also showing that removal of censoring alone can move the operating point toward greater false acceptance.

The prespecified recovery branches did not identify a deployable resolution within their frozen designs. TQ-EMA v1 did not meet its late-FRR recovery criterion. TQ-EMA v2 and the Adaptive Quantile Profile did not satisfy their validation gates, so outer test scores were not loaded and no outer estimates exist for those methods. Preserving these stopping outcomes limits the conclusions to mechanisms supported by the evidence rather than configurations selected after inspection. The McGill experiment then reproduced the directions of the boundary-poisoning, Guarded-security, and Oracle feedback effects under a different classifier, accelerometer-only inputs, and natural separation between collection days. It is a mechanism-level replication; its absolute error rates are not directly comparable with the HMOG results or evidence of benchmark performance.

The main implication is that access permission and learning permission should be treated as distinct design decisions. Separating them can substantially reduce malicious profile updates, yet making learning more conservative can also remove the genuine observations most needed for recovery. A practical adaptive authenticator may therefore require trusted recovery evidence that is not determined solely by the current access decision, together with bounded state movement and explicit monitoring of FAR, FRR, update composition, and future attack success. Larger untouched multi-day studies should evaluate such evidence, including step-up or multimodal corroboration and feature- or model-level adaptation, under benign temporal change and adversarial contamination in the same frozen protocol. The present findings define this joint evaluation problem; they do not claim a universal solution or an impossibility result for adaptive behavioral authentication.
