Behavioral continuous authentication can update a user's profile during operation, but using accepted observations for adaptation couples authentication errors to future profile state. This study investigates profile poisoning and accepted-only feedback censoring in score-level online behavioral authentication. The primary HMOG analysis compared Frozen, Blind exponential moving-average (EMA), Guarded EMA, and diagnostic Oracle-all-genuine profiles using held-out random and score-informed boundary poisoning, controlled synthetic claimant-only temporal shifts, and prespecified recovery alternatives. The principal mechanisms were then evaluated in an independent two-day McGill Gait replication.

Under the HMOG boundary attack at \(K=8\), post-poison attack success rate was 22.92% for Frozen, 53.96% for Blind EMA, and 22.93% for Guarded EMA. During the HMOG claimant-only shift at \(D=2\), accepted-only Guarded adaptation did not lower late false rejection rate relative to Frozen. Oracle-all restored rejected genuine observations to the update stream and improved late false rejection rate by 6.60 percentage points, but late false acceptance rate increased by 6.08 points. Prespecified quarantine/recovery and quantile-state alternatives did not establish improved false-rejection recovery under the frozen constraints. On McGill, boundary-attack success rates at \(K=8\) were 12.37% for Frozen, 32.09% for Blind EMA, and 12.48% for the transferred Guarded rule, directionally reproducing the security effect under natural day separation.

These results show under the tested protocols that separate update authorization can substantially reduce poisoning while leaving the access rule unchanged. They also support an authentication-specific selective-update bias: conservative accepted-only learning censors low-scoring genuine evidence needed for downward temporal recovery. Removing that censoring improves genuine tracking but can incur a large security cost. Poisoning resistance and adaptation utility should therefore be evaluated jointly, with trusted recovery evidence considered separately from ordinary authentication confidence.
